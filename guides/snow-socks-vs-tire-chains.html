Snow socks vs tire chains: the short version

Snow socks are fabric covers that slip over the tire. They are light, quiet, quick to fit and very unlikely to scratch wheels or hit suspension parts, which is why they suit low-clearance cars. The trade-off is durability: they are made for snow and ice, and driving on bare pavement wears them out fast. Think of them as a "get me through this stretch" tool.

Tire chains bite harder into packed snow and ice and last much longer, which matters if you cross mountain passes every weekend or have a heavy truck. They are noisier, rougher to drive on, and need more clearance around the tire. Cable chains sit in between: steel cables or alloy cross members in a much slimmer package that fits more passenger cars and SUVs.

How to choose

1. Start with your tire size, not the product

Every sock and chain on this page comes in many sizes, and each size fits a specific list of tire sizes. Read the full size off your tire sidewall (for example 235/65R17) and check it against the maker's fit chart on the listing. The link below each pick goes to one size; use the size selector on the listing to find yours. A device that is the wrong size will not stay centered and can come off.

2. Check your owner's manual for clearance and chain rules

Many modern cars and crossovers have very little room between the tire and the suspension, brake lines or fender liner. Their manuals often say to use only "SAE Class S" devices, or to avoid chains entirely. Class S is a clearance standard for low-profile traction devices. All five picks below state Class S compliance or limited-clearance design on their listings. The manual also tells you which wheels to put them on: usually the drive wheels, and for AWD vehicles, whatever the maker specifies.

3. Know the local rules where you drive

Chain laws vary by state and by road. Some only require chains or an approved alternative when signs are posted, and some accept textile socks as an alternative traction device while others may not on every road. Check the state transportation department's rules for the routes you drive before you rely on socks to get past a checkpoint.

4. Be honest about how often you will use them

If you need traction a few times a winter, socks are easier to live with: they fold into a small bag and go on in minutes with cold hands. If you drive steep, icy roads regularly or tow, chains are the more durable tool. Either way, they are not a substitute for winter tires if you see snow often.

5. Practice once in your driveway

The worst place to fit chains for the first time is a dark roadside in a storm. Do a dry run at home, keep a pair of gloves and a small mat with them, and store them where you can reach them without unloading the trunk. They belong in your winter car emergency kit alongside a snow brush and a flashlight.

Safety tip: traction aids are low-speed tools. Every listing we checked that gives a limit says 30 mph, and slower on rough or patchy roads. Take them off as soon as you are back on clear pavement. If you hear slapping or rubbing, stop and check the fit.

FAQ

Are snow socks as good as chains?

For light to moderate snow on paved roads, socks give a big improvement over bare tires and are much easier to use. On deep packed snow, steep grades or ice, and for repeated use, chains are generally the more capable and durable option. Many drivers with low-clearance cars choose socks because chains are not allowed by their owner's manual.

Do I need four or two?

The picks here are sold as sets of two, which is what most chain controls require on the drive wheels. Some manuals for AWD vehicles recommend a specific axle or all four wheels; follow your manual.

Can I drive on dry pavement with them?

Only briefly. Bare pavement wears socks out quickly and is hard on chains and tires. Remove them once the road is clear. If you get stuck with a flat or low tire on the same trip, a portable tire inflator is the other piece of gear worth carrying.

How to choose

1. Match the range to your vehicle

Most cars and SUVs run well under 50 PSI, so a 0-150 PSI gauge covers them with room to spare. If you drive a heavy truck or an RV, look at a gauge rated to 200 PSI or more.

2. Look at the stated accuracy

Listings usually quote a tolerance such as plus or minus 1 PSI, or cite the ANSI B40.7 standard. Remember these are the maker's claims. For the most useful results, use the same gauge every time so you can compare readings month to month.

3. Check the display and light

Winter mornings are dark. A backlit screen and a small light near the nozzle make it much easier to find the valve stem and read the number without a flashlight.

4. Think about cold-weather behavior

Batteries do not like extreme cold. Some listings mention a stated operating temperature range, which is worth a look if you live somewhere very cold. Keep the gauge in the cabin rather than a freezing trunk.

5. Decide between a gauge only and a gauge with inflator head

A plain gauge only reads pressure. A gauge with an inflator head attaches to an air compressor hose, so you can read and fill in one step. If you already own a compressor, or plan to get a portable tire inflator, that second type can be handy.

Practical tip: check pressure when the tires are cold, meaning the car has not been driven for a few hours, and compare it with the number on the sticker inside the driver's door jamb, not the number on the tire sidewall. Add a gauge to your winter car emergency kit.

FAQ

How often should I check tire pressure?

Many drivers check once a month and before long trips. Check more often when temperatures swing, since pressure drops as the air gets colder.

Which pressure should I use?

Use the number on the sticker in the driver's door jamb or in your owner's manual. The maximum figure printed on the tire sidewall is not the recommended pressure.

Are digital gauges better than stick gauges?

Digital ones are easier to read and often have a light, but they need batteries. A reading difference of a PSI or two between different gauges is common, so stick with one gauge.

What if a tire is low?

Top it up with a portable inflator and have a tire that keeps losing air looked at.

How to choose a tire inflator

1. Corded or cordless?

Corded 12V inflators plug into your car's accessory outlet. They are cheaper and cannot be "dead" when you need them, which matters in winter because lithium batteries lose capacity in the cold. Cordless models are more convenient for quick checks in the driveway, but you have to keep them charged. A good compromise is a cordless unit that can also run from a 12V cord.

2. Duty cycle

Small inflators heat up. Most list a maximum continuous run time (often around 10 to 15 minutes) followed by a cool-down. For topping up four tires by a few PSI this never matters. For filling a completely flat SUV tire, it does.

3. Auto shut-off and accuracy

Every pick here lets you set a target pressure and stops automatically. Even so, a separate gauge is a cheap way to double-check. Use the pressure on the sticker inside your driver's door jamb, not the maximum number printed on the tire sidewall.

4. Cord and hose length

On a corded unit you need enough reach to get from the front outlet to the rear tire on the far side. Around 10 feet of cord plus the air hose is enough for most cars; long SUVs and trucks benefit from more.

Cold weather tip: tire pressure drops as the temperature falls, roughly 1 PSI for every 10°F. Check pressure in the morning before you drive, when tires are "cold", for an accurate reading.

FAQ

Can a portable inflator fix a flat tire?

It can re-inflate a tire with a slow leak so you can drive to a shop. A tire with a puncture needs a plug or patch. See our winter car emergency kit checklist for a plug kit option.

Is a jump starter with a built-in inflator a good idea?

Combo units save space, but their inflators are usually slower than a dedicated one. If you buy a combo, check its continuous run time.

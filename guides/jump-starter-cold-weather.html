A portable jump starter lets you fix it yourself in a few minutes, with no second car and no waiting for roadside assistance. The catch: the jump starter itself also has to survive the cold. How to choose a jump starter for winter

1. Match it to your engine, not the biggest number

The headline "peak amps" figure is mostly marketing and is not measured the same way across brands. The more useful number is the engine size the maker says the unit can start, usually listed separately for gas and diesel. Cold weather is the stress test, so pick a unit rated comfortably above your engine. A 4-cylinder sedan is easy; a V8 pickup or a diesel needs a larger pack. If a listing gives cranking amps as well as peak amps, that is the better comparison.

2. Understand how each type handles cold

Lithium packs are small and light and hold a charge for months, which is why they are the most popular choice. Their weakness is temperature: output drops in deep cold, and lithium cells should not be charged below freezing. Supercapacitor units store energy in capacitors instead of a battery. They charge in minutes, usually from your weak car battery itself, and makers rate them for much lower temperatures. The trade-off is that they need some energy source to draw from first. Lead-acid (AGM) boxes are heavy, but they are the traditional shop tool, tolerate lots of use and have long, thick cables.

3. Check the operating temperature on the listing

Not every maker publishes one. When they do, it is the clearest sign of whether a unit is meant for winter. In the picks below we note the range only where the listing states it.

4. Think about where it will live

If the car sits outside overnight in sub-zero weather, a lithium pack left in the trunk is starting from a cold soak. Many drivers keep it in the cabin, or bring it indoors on the coldest nights. Whatever you buy, set a phone reminder to top it up every two to three months; a jump starter that is flat when you need it is just extra weight.

5. Extras are nice, but not the point

USB charging and a flashlight are genuinely useful when you are stuck at night. Built-in air compressors add bulk and are usually slower than a dedicated unit; our portable tire inflator guide covers that choice.

Safety tip: if the battery case is cracked, bulging, or you can see ice in it, do not jump it. A frozen battery can be damaged or dangerous when charged. Have it replaced instead. Follow the clamp order in your jump starter's manual, and wait between crank attempts rather than cranking continuously.

FAQ

Can I leave a lithium jump starter in the car all winter?

Many people do, but cold reduces its output, and lithium cells should be charged above freezing. If your car sits outside in sub-zero weather, keep the pack in the cabin or bring it inside on the coldest nights, and check its charge monthly. A supercapacitor unit is the alternative if that is not practical.

Is a jump starter better than jumper cables?

Cables are cheap and never need charging, but they need a second running car. A jump starter works alone, which is the whole point when you are in an empty parking lot at night. Many drivers carry both.

Will a jump starter fix a battery that keeps dying?

No. If your car needs a jump more than once in a few weeks, test the battery and charging system. If the car sits for long periods, a battery maintainer for winter storage prevents the drain in the first place. A jump starter also belongs in your winter car emergency kit.
